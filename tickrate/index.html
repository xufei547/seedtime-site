How many seconds a day does your mechanical watch gain or lose?

Tickrate is an iPhone app that tells you how many seconds a day your mechanical watch gains or loses.

It compares the watch with atomic time a few hours or days apart for its true daily rate, and it can listen to the ticking through the iPhone's microphone for a quick estimate, always shown as a range.

One watch is free, with unlimited checks and listens; its history shows the last 30 days. Tickrate Unlimited, a one-time purchase (US$7.99 in the United States), adds every watch, the full history, export and import.

How it works

Atomic time checks: the true rate

Open a watch and tap Check. Tickrate shows atomic time; you tap the moment your second hand reaches 12. Check again hours or days later and Tickrate draws a line through your checks: its slope is the daily rate, with a range. Checks a day apart give about ±0.4 seconds a day; a week apart, much less.

Listening: a quick estimate

Touch the bottom edge of your iPhone to the watch case next to the crown, in a quiet room, for 20 to 60 seconds. Tickrate measures the ticking two independent ways and shows the rate as a range, with what the range is made of. If the two ways disagree, it shows no number and says why.

Calibrate this iPhone, once

Each iPhone's audio clock runs a little fast or slow, and that shifts every listen. Leave Tickrate open on the charger for about an hour and it measures your iPhone's clock against atomic time and corrects every later listen.

What Tickrate can't do

  • Listening needs a quiet room and the iPhone touching the case. Some watches tick too quietly for a phone microphone.
  • Quartz and other electronic watches can't be measured by listening. Atomic time checks work with any watch that has a seconds hand.
  • A listen is an estimate of the rate at that moment, in that position. The true daily rate comes from atomic time checks over a day or more.
  • It measures the rate only, not amplitude or beat error.
  • Until you calibrate your iPhone (about an hour on the charger), listening ranges are wider.
  • iPhone only, iOS 17 or later.

Questions

Can an iPhone tell if a mechanical watch runs fast or slow?

Yes. Tickrate does it two ways: comparing the watch with atomic time a few hours or days apart gives its true daily rate, about ±0.4 seconds a day with checks a day apart. Listening to the ticking for 20 to 60 seconds gives a quick estimate of the current rate, shown as a range.

How do I check my watch's accuracy without a timegrapher?

Compare it with an accurate clock at two moments and divide the difference by the time between them. Tickrate does this for you: it shows atomic time, you tap when your second hand reaches 12, and after the second check it shows the rate in seconds per day. Your reaction time is about the same each time, so it mostly cancels out.

How many seconds a day is normal for a mechanical watch?

It depends on the movement; anything from a few seconds to a few tens of seconds a day is common. A certified chronometer must average between −4 and +6 seconds a day in the COSC test. Widely used affordable automatic movements are specified more loosely: Seiko's NH35, for example, at −20 to +40 seconds a day. Position, how fully wound the watch is and temperature move the rate by seconds a day, so one reading is a snapshot.

How accurate is measuring with the iPhone microphone?

On a clean signal from a modern watch, a 30-second listen gives a range about ±0.5 to ±1 second a day wide, once the iPhone's own clock is calibrated. Each iPhone's audio clock runs slightly fast or slow (about 2 seconds a day on the iPhone we measured), so before calibrating Tickrate widens every range to about ±3 seconds a day instead of hiding that error. These figures come from our tests on recordings of real watches and synthetic ticking, not from a large set of iPhones.

Why does Tickrate show a range instead of one number?

Because one exact-looking number would claim more than a short listen can know. The range is built from what can move the result: the scatter between ticks, the difference between two independent measuring methods, the watch changing while it is heard, and the iPhone's own clock. The app shows how much each part adds, and when the two methods disagree it shows no number at all.

What if Tickrate can't hear my watch?

It says so, gives no number, and tells you why. Try a quiet room, the watch dial up on something soft, and the bottom edge of the iPhone, where the microphone is, touching the case next to the crown. If it still can't hear it, use atomic time checks: they work with any watch that has a seconds hand, in any room.

Does Tickrate need an internet connection?

Only for atomic time. Checks and calibrating the iPhone read the time from public time servers: Apple's first, Cloudflare's or Google's if Apple's doesn't answer. Listening works offline, and without a connection a check uses the iPhone's own clock and says so.

Does Tickrate record or upload sound?

No. The microphone is used only while you listen to a watch or calibrate the iPhone, and the sound is analysed on the iPhone and never recorded, saved or uploaded. There is no account; your watches and results stay on your iPhone, and you can export them as CSV or a backup file.

How does Tickrate compare with a timegrapher?

A timegrapher measures more: rate, amplitude and beat error in each position, with a contact microphone; Tickrate measures only the rate. What Tickrate adds is the long view: atomic time checks over days give the average rate as you actually wear the watch. If you regulate movements or need amplitude and beat error, use a timegrapher.

Which watches does Tickrate work with?

Atomic time checks work with any watch that has a seconds hand, mechanical or quartz. Listening works with mechanical watches at standard beat rates from 12,000 to 43,200 beats per hour, such as 21,600 and 28,800; Tickrate can detect the beat rate itself. Quartz watches can't be measured by listening.
